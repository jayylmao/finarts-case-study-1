Executive Summary

This report studies what moves WTI crude oil returns. It uses 96 monthly observations from January 2018 to December 2025. It asks three questions. Which global financial conditions are associated with WTI returns? Do Philippine macro conditions add explanatory power beyond them? Do these relationships change in market-stress months?

Two global conditions hold up under every check. A 1% S&P 500 return goes with a 0.65 point higher WTI return. A 1 percentage point rise in the 10-year Treasury yield goes with a 14.6 point higher return. Both stay significant with ordinary and robust standard errors. The dollar and the VIX carry the expected negative signs, but neither is significant once March and May 2020 are modeled separately.

Philippine inflation, the peso and manufacturing output add nothing. The nested F-test does not reject (p 0.278).

The initial model failed its diagnostics. The failures came from two COVID months, March and May 2020. Event dummies for those months fix the functional form, normality and heteroskedasticity problems. The improved model uses global conditions plus the two dummies.

Fit is limited. In ordinary months, the global model explains 22.4% of WTI return variation (adjusted R-squared). Oil-specific supply and demand shocks explain the rest.

In stress months, the joint test of equal slopes does not reject (p 0.472).

Introduction

Oil is a global commodity. Its price reflects global growth, the value of the dollar and the appetite for risk. The Philippines is a net oil importer, so the price can matter for local inflation and the peso. This report tests whether the usual financial conditions explain monthly WTI returns, and whether Philippine conditions add anything.

The sample runs from January 2018 to December 2025. It includes the 2020 oil collapse. On 6 March 2020, OPEC+ talks in Vienna failed after Russia refused deeper cuts. Saudi Arabia then cut its selling prices and the price war began, just as COVID-19 lockdowns cut demand. On 12 April 2020, OPEC+ agreed to cut output by 9.7 million barrels per day from 1 May. The sample also covers the 2022 rate-hiking cycle of the Federal Reserve. These episodes make oil returns volatile and far from normal. The report treats this as a core modeling problem.

The research question is: which global financial conditions are associated with WTI monthly log returns during 2018 to 2025, do Philippine macro conditions add explanatory power beyond them, and do these relationships change in market-stress months?

The report proceeds in six steps. The Data and Variables section describes the nine monthly series and the checks on them. The Initial Regression Model section estimates a global model and a global plus Philippine model. The Diagnostics section tests the OLS assumptions. The Model Improvement section applies robust standard errors and event dummies. The Advanced Regression section tests the stress-month question. The last sections interpret the results and give recommendations.

Unit of observation and sample

The unit of observation is one calendar month, from January 2018 to December 2025. To build a regression model, every variable must be measured on the same unit of observation. CPI, BSP Peso per USD, and gold are only available monthly, thus we collapse the daily FRED and Yahoo! Finance series to monthly to match. It is worth noting that this sacrifices the detail that daily observations would grant us, and limits us to 96 observations over the sample period.

First, we start by getting the last non-missing price per month in the WTI and get the log return. FRED leaves U.S. market holidays blank, so na.omit drops those days before we take the month-end value.

wti_m <- apply.monthly(na.omit(wti), last)

wti_ret <- diff(log(wti_m))

Unlike the other global variables, we keep it as a level (the monthly average, as the case brief specifies) instead of a change: the question is how stressed the market was during the month, and the average captures that better than a single month-end reading.

Variable definitions

The remaining series are brought onto the same monthly footing as the rest. For the BSP rate we use the end-of-month value so that it lines up with the WTI closing price, and a positive log change means the peso weakened. The PSA volume of production index swings with the calendar (over 2010 to 2019 it fell about 6% on average every January and rose about 6% every March), so rather than a month-on-month change we use its 12-month log change, which is also how the CPI enters as year-on-year inflation. Log returns and growth rates are expressed in percent so that coefficients read as “percentage points of WTI return per unit of the regressor”.

The expected signs come from how each variable is linked to the oil market. A “?” means that there are channels pointing in both directions.

The manufacturing variable has extreme values: a 12-month log change of -172.5% in Aug 2020 and 184.7% in Jul 2021. These are not data errors. One timing difference is worth noting. Gold enters as the return on WGC monthly average prices, while WTI and the other market series use month-end prices. Returns computed from averages are smoother and lag behind month-end returns, which weakens any same-month relationship between gold and oil.

In the full model, equity returns, the dollar, Treasury yields and the VIX are all significant at the 5% level. Each coefficient is read ceteris paribus, that is, holding the other regressors constant. On that basis, a 1% rise in the S&P 500 is associated with a 0.85 percentage point higher WTI return, and a 1% rise in the broad dollar with a 2.83 point lower one. Oil moves with risk appetite and against the currency it is priced in. With the other variables fixed, a one point rise in the VIX goes with a 0.45 point lower return. The yield coefficient is large and positive (19.3 points per percentage point of yield change), which reads more naturally as yields and oil both responding to growth news than as yields pushing oil up. Gold is not significant once the other conditions are in. These signs match the expectations in the variable table.

The overall F-test rejects the null that all eight slope coefficients are zero (F = 7.68, p < 0.001), so the regressors jointly explain WTI returns.

Statistical inference

For each coefficient we test H₀: β = 0 (no association with WTI returns, holding the others constant) against the two-sided H₁: β ≠ 0, at the 5% level. The table below adds the 95% confidence interval for each coefficient, and the change in WTI return implied by a typical (one standard deviation) move in each regressor. That last column measures economic significance. For comparison, the standard deviation of WTI returns is 13.6 percentage points.

The S&P 500, dollar, yield, VIX and peso coefficients reject H₀ at 5%, and their confidence intervals exclude zero. Gold, CPI and manufacturing do not, and their intervals comfortably include zero.

A small p-value says an effect is unlikely to be zero, not that it is large, so we look at both. Here the statistically significant global factors are also economically large: a typical one standard deviation move in the S&P 500, the dollar or Treasury yields shifts the expected WTI return by roughly 4 to 5 percentage points, about a third of WTI’s own monthly standard deviation. But the intervals are wide. The dollar’s interval runs from -4.8 to -0.9, so the data cannot tell a small dollar effect from a large one. The manufacturing coefficient shows the reverse case: it looks tiny (-0.028 per point), but because vopi swings so widely its one standard deviation effect is comparable to gold’s, and it is still not statistically different from zero. A coefficient’s size alone therefore says little until it is scaled by how much the regressor actually moves.

diag_battery <- function(m) {

  tests <- list(
    "Functional form"    = list("RESET (fitted^2, fitted^3)" = resettest(m, power = 2:3, type = "fitted")),
    "Normality"          = list("Shapiro-Wilk"   = shapiro.test(resid(m)),
                                "Jarque-Bera"    = jarque.bera.test(resid(m))),

Model Improvement

The diagnostics point to two related problems:

  1. Unreliable standard errors. Heteroskedasticity (error variance that is not constant across months) is borderline, and the residuals are far from normal. Either way, the usual OLS standard errors, and the p-values built on them, cannot be taken at face value.
  2. Two months dominate the fit. March and May 2020 are the source of the RESET, normality and influence failures, and they carry much of the apparent R-squared.

Multicollinearity and autocorrelation are not problems, so they need no remedy. We address the two problems in turn and keep the original model for comparison.

Remedy 1: heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors

Robust (heteroskedasticity-consistent, or HC) standard errors estimate each coefficient’s uncertainty without assuming a constant error variance. They leave the coefficients themselves unchanged. We use the HC3 version, which is recommended for small samples because it gives more weight to the residuals of high-leverage observations, exactly the situation March 2020 creates.

Remedy 2: respecify the model with event dummies for March and May 2020

Deleting the two months would throw away real data without saying why. Instead we add a dummy variable for each month: a variable equal to 1 in that month and 0 in every other. Each dummy absorbs its month’s return completely, so the slope coefficients are estimated from the other 94 months. The dummy coefficients then report how far each month’s return was from what financial conditions alone would predict.

This is justified by what happened in the oil market itself, not just by the statistics. In March 2020, the OPEC+ talks collapsed on 6 March and Saudi Arabia started a price war just as lockdowns cut oil demand. In May 2020, prices rebounded as OPEC+’s record production cut took effect and economies began to reopen. These were supply decisions and physical demand shocks specific to oil, which equity returns, the dollar, yields and the VIX are not designed to capture.

The adjusted R-squared of the improved model (65.6%) should not be read as a better explanation of oil returns. The dummies fit their two months perfectly, and those months contain much of WTI’s total variation, so the R-squared jumps for a purely mechanical reason. Our choice of the improved model rests instead on the diagnostics, the F-test on the Philippine block and the information criteria.

The comparison has four implications:

  • Two relationships survive every check: Holding the other conditions constant, a 1% S&P 500 return goes with a 0.65 point higher WTI return (initially 0.85), and a 1 percentage point rise in the 10-year yield with a 14.6 point higher return (initially 19.3). Both are significant with ordinary and robust standard errors and are smaller than in the initial model, which had been inflated by the COVID months.
  • The dollar and VIX effects are fragile: Both keep the expected negative sign, but they shrink (dollar -1.04, VIX -0.17) and are not significant (p 0.132 and 0.269). Their significance in the initial model came almost entirely from March and May 2020.
  • Philippine conditions add nothing once the global conditions are known: This answers the second part of the research question.
  • Most monthly oil-price variation is oil-specific: In ordinary months, financial conditions explain only about a fifth of WTI returns, and in the two COVID months the gap between actual and predicted returns was 50 to 60 percentage points. Supply decisions and physical demand shocks, which these regressors do not measure, drive most of the rest.

The dummies also have a cost for the next step: the model now says nothing about March and May 2020 themselves. Any test of whether the relationships change in stress months in the Advanced Regression section therefore has to rely on the other high-VIX months, and should check that its result is not just a restatement of these two.

Advanced Regression

The last part of the research question asks whether the relationships change in market-stress months. We test this with interaction terms. A stress dummy equals 1 in months when the VIX is in its top quartile and 0 otherwise. Interacting the dummy with each regressor lets the slopes differ in stress months. The VIX level enters on its own, because the stress dummy is built from it.

Financial Interpretation

This section reads the improved model, m_global_d. It holds the five global conditions and the March and May 2020 dummies. All coefficients are associations in one sample. They are not causal effects.

Equity returns. Oil moves with risk appetite. A 1% S&P 500 return goes with a 0.65 point higher WTI return. A one standard deviation move in the index (4.8 points) shifts the expected WTI return by 3.1 points. The link survives robust standard errors. Strong equity months signal stronger growth and stronger oil demand.

Treasury yields. A 1 percentage point rise in the 10-year yield goes with a 14.6 point higher WTI return. This does not mean higher borrowing costs lift oil. Growth news probably moves both. Yields work here as a growth signal.

The dollar. The sign is negative, as expected. Oil is priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar makes it dearer for other buyers. The coefficient is -1.04 and is not significant (p 0.132). The data cannot tell a small dollar effect from zero. The dollar looked strong in the initial model only because of March and May 2020.

The VIX. The sign is negative, as expected. Higher uncertainty goes with risk-off selling. The coefficient is -0.17 and is not significant (p 0.269). Its earlier significance also came from the two COVID months.

Gold. Gold is not significant. One reason is timing. Gold enters as a return on monthly average prices, while WTI uses month-end prices. This weakens any same-month link.

Philippine conditions. CPI inflation, the peso and manufacturing output are not jointly significant. The Philippines is a price taker in oil. Any link runs from oil to local prices and the peso, not the other way. The peso looked significant in the initial model, but that result more plausibly reflects the reverse direction and its link to the dollar. For Philippine firms and policymakers, oil is an external shock. Local conditions do not help explain it.

Event months. The March 2020 dummy is -52.7 and the May 2020 dummy is 58.9 in the full model. Even after accounting for equities, the dollar, yields and the VIX, oil moved far more than financial conditions predict in those months. The causes were oil-specific. They were the OPEC+ breakdown and price war in March, and the record production cut in May.

Fit. In ordinary months, the global model explains 22.4% of WTI return variation (adjusted). Financial conditions are a weak guide to monthly oil returns. Supply decisions and physical demand shocks drive most of the rest.

Reliability. The initial standard errors were unreliable because two months dominated the fit. The improved model passes the diagnostics. It still rests on 96 observations. It also says nothing about March and May 2020, since the dummies absorb them.

Stress months. The stress-month model adds interactions for the S&P 500, dollar, yield and gold. The joint test finds no evidence that the slopes change in the top-quartile VIX months (p 0.472). Only 22 stress months remain after the two COVID dummies, so the test has limited power. For an investor, this means the S&P 500 and yield links found in the full sample are a fair guide in high-VIX months too, but the data cannot rule out a change.

Recommendation and Conclusion

Recommendations

  1. Use the S&P 500 and the 10-year yield as the main financial co-movement signals for oil. They are the only two global conditions that stay significant under every check.

  2. Do not rely on the dollar or the VIX to explain monthly oil returns. Their effects are not distinguishable from zero outside March and May 2020.

  3. Do not add Philippine CPI, the peso or manufacturing output to an oil return model. They add no explanatory power. Treat oil as an external shock to Philippine inflation and the peso.

  4. Model event months separately. Use event dummies for extreme months and report robust standard errors. Do not delete the months, since they are real market events.

  5. Do not use this model alone to forecast oil. It explains about 22.4% of ordinary-month variation. Add oil-specific variables such as inventories and OPEC+ output decisions in future work.

Conclusion

Three findings answer the research question. First, the S&P 500 and the 10-year yield are the global conditions associated with WTI returns. The dollar and the VIX are not robust. Second, Philippine macro conditions add no explanatory power beyond global ones. Third, the joint test of equal slopes in stress and calm months does not reject (p 0.472).

The initial OLS model overstated both fit and significance. Two COVID months drove its results. Event dummies and robust standard errors give a model that passes every diagnostic. The limits are clear. The sample has 96 months. The results are associations. Most oil price variation comes from factors outside the model.
